1. In recent years, visual generation is rapidly evolving beyond conventional image synthesis into a general medium for visual creation, spanning multilingual typography, information-dense design, high-resolution rendering, multi-reference composition, and fine-grained object-background editing, and interleaved generation. However, this broader scope exposes a fundamental architectural divide: most systems perceive images through pretrained vision encoders (VEs) [108, 152, 97, 119] but generate them through variational autoencoders (VAEs) [55, 145, 58, 106]. Consequently, understanding and generation operate in distinct representational spaces: one optimized for semantic abstraction, the other for pixel-level fidelity. Although effective, this separation limits how seamlessly perception, reasoning, and generation can be coordinated within a unified visual framework across diverse forms of visual creation.   Native unified modelling takes a different route by learning directly from pixels and words. SenseNova-U1 [29] established the feasibility of this paradigm through the encoder-free and VAE-free NEO-unify [101] architecture, bringing perception, reasoning, and pixel-space generation into a single end-to-end model. Its deliberately lightweight visual interface, however, reconstructs each visual token as an independent RGB patch. Although efficient, this factorization prevents information exchange across neighbouring patches during the final stage of image formation, making seams, texture discontinuities, and geometric inconsistencies increasingly pronounced at high resolutions.   Here we present SenseNova-U1.5, an 8B-MoT native unified multimodal model for visual understanding, reasoning, and generation. At its core is a first major architectural shift: from independent patch prediction to spatially joint reconstruction. Rather than mapping each visual token to pixels in isolation, we project tokens onto a two-dimensional feature field and progressively reconstruct the image through spatial convolutions and Pixel Shuffle upsampling. This allows neighbouring regions to exchange information before pixels are finalized, so colour, texture, and geometry can be resolved jointly rather than patch by patch. The resulting design preserves the efficiency of compact visual sequences while substantially improving spatial coherence and enabling native generation at resolutions of up to 4K.   The second major advance is a shift from joint reward optimization to a specialize-then-unify strategy. Visual creation spans fundamentally different capabilities, from aesthetic synthesis and bilingual text rendering to infographic design and image editing, each with distinct reward signals, rollout dynamics, and optimization challenges. Jointly optimizing them within a single policy can entangle competing objectives and dilute task-specific gains.

The infographic expert first undergoes a complete training process from infographic-oriented mid-training to task-specific RL, aiming to achieve more substantial improvements in small-text rendering and related capabilities. During mid-training, we introduce high-quality real and synthetic data and rebalance both the infographic subcategories and the mixture of infographic and other categories of data. This further improves small-text rendering, complex layout handling, information organization, and overall visual design quality.   Here, we further refine the infographic expert in three stages. The first improves text rendering using the same training data and text-fidelity reward as the OCR expert. The second applies direct preference optimization (DPO) [61] to curated preference pairs to enhance overall visual quality, using a constant learning rate of $5\times 10^{-6}$, a DPO loss coefficient of $\beta=10$, and one timestep sampled from steps 1–20 at each update. The final stage alternates text-rendering and aesthetic data, routing them separately to the text-fidelity reward and HPSv3++ [76], rather than merging the two scores. 3. Stage 5: Multi-Expert On-Policy Distillation. In Figure [4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.2 Training Procedure ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ SenseNova-U1.5: Towards Native Unified Visual Intelligence"), we apply on-policy velocity-field distillation to consolidate four specialized experts for aesthetic quality, text rendering, infographic generation, and image editing into a single unified model. Each training sample is hard-routed to the frozen expert corresponding to its capability. Given a text-only or text-image condition $\mathbf{c}$ from capability $m$, the student first generates its own trajectory $\{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta,t}\}_{t=0}^{1}$. The student and routed expert are then evaluated at the same student-generated state, timestep, and condition:   Equation $$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\\|\mathbf{v}_{\theta}\!\left(\operatorname{sg}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta,t}),t,\mathbf{c}\right)-\mathbf{v}_{m}\!\left(\operatorname{sg}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta,t}),t,\mathbf{c}\right)\right\\|_{2}^{2}\right]. $$   Here, $\operatorname{sg}$ denotes stop-gradient, preventing gradients from propagating through the full sampling trajectory. Each epoch uses a single capability partition and its paired frozen expert, rotating in a fixed order.   Both text-to-image generation and image editing use fixed 30-step deterministic ODE denoising steps, with one queried timestep per trajectory. To learn both high-noise states that establish global structure and low-noise states that determine local details, we progressively shift the query distribution over training. At the $n$-th epoch, the continuous query location and its discrete 0-based index are determined by $t_{e}\sim\operatorname{Beta}\!\left(2+3n/N,\,5-3n/N\right)$, where $N$ is the total number of epochs. The distribution therefore moves smoothly from $\operatorname{Beta}(2,5)$, which emphasizes the early high-noise portion of the trajectory, to $\operatorname{Beta}(5,2)$, which emphasizes late low-noise states and hence fine details and text fidelity. To align training and inference, we directly optimize the velocity under classifier-free guidance (CFG). We use scale $s=4$ for the aesthetic, OCR, and infographic experts, with global-norm clipping, and scale $s=1$ for the editing expert.

During post-training, aesthetic and OCR prompts use their respective rewards and are interleaved at the epoch level, avoiding the need to combine heterogeneous reward scales within each sample. Notably, GRPO and DAPO improve efficiency and scalability by removing explicit value models and introducing group-relative advantages and enhanced online optimization strategies [102, 147]. Online RL has also been extended to visual generation, with ReFL, DDPO, and DPOK optimizing diffusion models via preference rewards or policy gradients [140, 5, 35], while AlignProp and D3PO improve efficiency and reduce reliance on explicit reward models [94, 144]. More recently, Flow-GRPO and DanceGRPO extend group-relative optimization to diffusion and flow-matching models, improving preference alignment, compositional accuracy, text rendering, and visual quality [74, 142].   For flow-matching models, online RL requires stochastic exploration beyond deterministic ordinary differential equation (ODE) inference. Flow-GRPO [73] enables such exploration through stochastic differential equation (SDE)-based rollouts, while coefficients-preserving sampling (CPS) [121] and Precise [163] improve finite-step sampling by better preserving the underlying flow dynamics and balancing exploration with distributional fidelity. Beyond sampling, GRPO-Guard [124] mitigates reward over-optimization through regulated clipping and noise-aware gradient reweighting. Existing visual RL further relies on capability-specific rewards, including preference models for perceptual quality and text–image alignment [140, 133, 86, 76], as well as specialized rewards for typography and editing [52, 47]. Motivated by these advances, we adopt task-dependent RL post-training that combines CPS or Precise sampling with interleaved or task-specific rewards to address the distinct optimization requirements of generation and editing.

## Response

# SenseNova-U1.5’s “specialize-then-unify” strategy

SenseNova-U1.5 does **not** try to optimize all visual-generation abilities with one reinforcement-learning (RL) objective from the beginning. Instead, it follows two phases:

1. **Specialize:** train separate experts for different visual abilities.
2. **Unify:** transfer those experts’ capabilities into one model through multi-expert on-policy distillation. [4]

## 1. Why joint RL optimization is difficult

The model must handle substantially different tasks, including aesthetic image generation, bilingual text rendering, infographic design, and image editing. These tasks have different notions of success, reward signals, rollout behaviors, and optimization requirements. [1]

A single jointly optimized policy can therefore create two problems:

- **Competing objectives become entangled.** For example, maximizing general aesthetic quality may harm text legibility, while maximizing text fidelity does not necessarily improve overall visual appearance. [5]
- **Task-specific improvements can be diluted.** When the same update must serve several heterogeneous capabilities, progress useful for one task may be weakened by objectives from another task. The paper describes this as entangling competing objectives and diluting task-specific gains. [1]

The paper’s evidence explains the practical motivation, but it does **not** provide a separate quantitative experiment proving that joint RL fails in every case. Its claim is an architectural and optimization motivation rather than a fully isolated ablation result in the supplied evidence. [1]

## 2. How the specialized experts are trained

Before RL specialization, the model undergoes unified mid-training and supervised fine-tuning. These stages combine understanding, text-to-image generation, image editing, and interleaved image-text data, so the starting model already has shared multimodal capabilities. [6]

During the multi-expert RL stage, four experts are trained:

- an **aesthetic expert**,
- an **OCR/text-rendering expert**,
- an **infographic expert**, and
- an **image-editing expert**. [6]

Each expert receives task-specific data and uses task-dependent rewards, sampling procedures, and regularization. [6]

### Aesthetic expert

The aesthetic expert alternates between aesthetic-preference data and typography data. Aesthetic samples use HPSv3++ to evaluate perceptual quality and prompt–image alignment, while typography samples use a bilingual OCR reward to evaluate text fidelity. The rewards are routed separately instead of being mixed into one score. [5]

The OCR reward compares the target text with text recognized from the generated image. It uses word-level matching for English and character-level matching for Chinese, with a multiset intersection-over-union score that penalizes omissions, duplications, and rendering errors. [5][7]

The aesthetic expert generates 16 candidates per prompt over a 30-step trajectory. It uses guidance scale 4, timestep shift 3, coefficients-preserving sampling with \(\eta=0.7\), and optimization focused on an early five-step window. The final generation Transformer block, flow-matching output head, and output-normalization layer are frozen during RL to reduce reward-induced drift. [5]

### OCR/text-rendering expert

The OCR expert is specialized for accurate bilingual text rendering. It extracts the intended text from the prompt, applies PaddleOCR to the generated image, and computes the normalized multiset overlap between the target and recognized text. This graded reward allows partially correct text to receive partial credit while remaining sensitive to missing, repeated, or malformed text. [5]

The supplied evidence also reports that this expert uses 16 candidates, 30-step trajectories, guidance scale 4, a five-step optimization window from the first ten steps, Precise sampling, and GRPO-Guard for stabilization under sparse high OCR rewards. [9]

### Infographic expert

The infographic expert is first trained from infographic-oriented mid-training through task-specific RL. Its data mixture is rebalanced using high-quality real and synthetic examples to improve small-text rendering, dense layouts, information organization, and overall design quality. [2]

Its later training has three stages:

1. improve text rendering with the OCR expert’s data and text-fidelity reward;
2. apply DPO to curated preference pairs for overall visual quality; and
3. alternate text-rendering and aesthetic data, routing them separately to the text-fidelity reward and HPSv3++. [2]

The first and third stages use GRPO with 16 candidates and 30-step trajectories, while classifier-free guidance is used for rollout reward evaluation rather than for the policy objective itself. [2]

### Image-editing expert

The editing expert is designed to balance three requirements: obeying the editing instruction, preserving unedited content, and maintaining visual quality in the edited regions. [9]

It uses a VLM-based reward with five dimensions:

- instruction fulfillment,
- edit execution,
- overall visual quality,
- text-editing quality when relevant, and
- preservation of unedited regions. [9]

It also uses progressive sliding-window training: optimization moves from early trajectory stages, which establish semantic structure, toward later stages, which refine texture and local integration. The paper avoids SDE-based trajectory sampling for this expert because excessive stochastic perturbation can introduce residual noise and artifacts. [9]

## 3. How the capabilities are consolidated

After the experts have been separately optimized, SenseNova-U1.5 performs **multi-expert on-policy distillation**. The goal is to transfer the complementary strengths of the experts into one unified policy. [3][4]

The procedure works as follows:

1. **Freeze the experts.** The four specialized experts are kept fixed during this consolidation stage. [3]
2. **Route each sample by capability.** A training sample is hard-routed to the frozen expert corresponding to its task, such as OCR, aesthetics, infographics, or editing. [3]
3. **Let the student generate its own trajectory.** Given a text-only or text-image condition, the unified student model produces its own generation trajectory. [3]
4. **Evaluate both models at the same student-generated state.** The student and the routed expert are compared at the same state, timestep, and condition. [3]
5. **Match velocity fields.** The student minimizes the squared difference between its velocity prediction and the expert’s velocity prediction:
   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}
   =
   \mathbb{E}\left[
   \left\|
   \mathbf v_\theta(\operatorname{sg}(\hat{\mathbf x}_{\theta,t}),t,\mathbf c)
   -
   \mathbf v_m(\operatorname{sg}(\hat{\mathbf x}_{\theta,t}),t,\mathbf c)
   \right\|_2^2
   \right].
   \]
   The stop-gradient operation prevents gradients from propagating through the complete sampling trajectory. [3]
6. **Rotate through the experts.** Each epoch uses one capability partition and its paired frozen expert, with the capability partitions visited in a fixed order. [3]
7. **Cover both coarse and fine generation states.** The queried timestep distribution gradually shifts from early, high-noise states to late, low-noise states. This teaches the student both global structure and local details such as fine textures and text fidelity. [3]
8. **Match inference conditions.** The student directly optimizes velocity under classifier-free guidance, using scale 4 for the aesthetic, OCR, and infographic experts, and scale 1 for the editing expert. [3]

In short, the unified model learns from each expert **on the student’s own trajectories**, rather than merely copying independent expert outputs. This is intended to consolidate the experts’ abilities into a single policy while preserving their complementary strengths. [4]

## Contradictions and limitations

There is no direct contradiction in the supplied evidence about the overall strategy: both the summary and the training description state that specialized experts are trained first and then consolidated by on-policy distillation. [4][6]

There is, however, a possible wording tension:

- The paper describes the experts as using “tailored” or task-specific rewards. [4][6]
- For the infographic expert, it explicitly says that no additional infographic-specific reward model is introduced; instead, the expert reuses the OCR and aesthetic rewards through stage- and task-conditioned routing. [7]

These statements can be reconciled: “task-specific” means that reward selection and routing are adapted to the expert’s training stage and task, not necessarily that every expert has a completely unique reward model. [2][7]

The supplied evidence also does not specify how much capability is retained from each expert after distillation, nor does it provide a detailed comparison against a fully joint-RL baseline. Those details are therefore not established by the provided paper chunks.
